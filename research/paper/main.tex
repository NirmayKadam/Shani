\begin{abstract}
Index options on the National Stock Exchange of India (NSE) are traded mostly by retail participants, and most of them rely on indicators such as the Relative Strength Index (RSI) and Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD). Institutional desks price the same contracts with model-based fair values. We ask a narrow question: if we price NIFTY~50 options with a Crank-Nicolson finite-difference solver and trade the gap between model and market price, does that gap survive Indian transaction costs, and how does it compare with an RSI/MACD strategy?

A solver fed each strike's own implied volatility reproduces market prices by construction, so we price every strike from a natural cubic-spline volatility surface fitted across strikes instead. Costs are modelled explicitly: STT, exchange and SEBI charges, stamp duty, GST, brokerage and a bid-ask allowance. The data are 180 trading days of NSE F\&O Bhavcopy records, December 2023 to June 2024. We compare a Retail Baseline, a Pure PDE strategy and a Hybrid strategy that adds momentum filters to the PDE signal.

Over the full sample the Pure PDE strategy returned $+49.57\%$ after costs (Sharpe $2.07$, Sortino $5.09$), while the Retail Baseline lost $5.58\%$ (Sharpe $-0.40$). The PDE strategy was profitable in all three India VIX regimes, best in the middle one ($+21.44\%$, Sharpe $3.28$), had Sharpe above $2.0$ in each of four walk-forward folds, and still returned $+34.74\%$ (Sharpe $1.43$) with costs tripled. The sample is short and covers one market episode, so we treat these figures as evidence that the signal deserves a longer test, not as proof of a durable edge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{N}{SE} is the largest derivatives exchange in the world by contract count, and weekly NIFTY~50 and BANKNIFTY options account for a large share of that volume. SEBI's 2023 study of individual traders \cite{sebi2023study} found that approximately 89\% of them lost money in equity derivatives. The average loss was roughly INR~50,000 per trader, and transaction costs added about 28\% on top of it. These costs include Securities Transaction Tax (STT), exchange levies, stamp duty, and GST.

Most of these traders work from chart indicators: RSI \cite{wilder1978new}, MACD \cite{appel2005technical} and Bollinger Bands \cite{bollinger2001bollinger}. An indicator reads the past path of the underlying and does not indicate whether the option itself is cheap or expensive. Pricing desks start at the opposite end. They compute the fair value from the Black-Scholes-Merton framework \cite{black1973pricing, merton1973theory}, often with finite-difference methods such as Crank-Nicolson \cite{crank1947practical} and compared it with the quote.

The finite-difference literature \cite{duffy2006finite, wilmott2006paul} is mostly about accuracy: convergence order, boundary treatment, and grid design. We did not find a study that takes the resulting model-versus-market gap, charges it the full set of Indian statutory costs, and compares the outcome with a plain retail indicator strategy. This paper is a small attempt at that comparison.

The system grew out of a few problems we ran into along the way. Our first version priced each option with its own implied volatility, and the model price came back equal to the market price every time, so there was nothing to trade. We now fit a natural cubic spline to the day's implied volatilities across strikes and price each strike from that curve, $\sigma_{\text{surface}}(K)$; deviations from the curve are what we call mispricing. The solver is Crank-Nicolson with the system matrix factorized once using SuperLU, since the matrix does not change within a pricing call. On the cost side, we coded the STT, exchange, stamp duty, SEBI, GST, and brokerage rules one by one, added a bid-ask allowance, and used the total as a per-trade threshold $\epsilon_t$ that a mispricing has to exceed before we enter. Finally, we tried a hybrid variant that also asks MACD and RSI to agree, to see whether that removes PDE signals firing against a strong trend.

An earlier version of the project also included a CNN-LSTM price predictor and a FinBERT sentiment module. We removed both and kept a purely model-based pipeline because \fillin{authors: state the real reason, e.g., results were not reproducible, signals could not be explained, latency}.

The remainder of this paper covers related work (Section~II), the solver and volatility surface (III), the cost model (IV), the strategies (V), data and backtester (VI), results (VII), and conclusions (VIII).

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Finite-Difference Methods in Option Pricing}
Schwartz \cite{schwartz1977valuation} was among the first to solve the option-pricing PDE with implicit finite differences. The Crank-Nicolson scheme was developed for heat conduction \cite{crank1947practical} and later became a standard tool in finance because it is second-order accurate in both space and time at modest cost \cite{duffy2006finite}. Wilmott \cite{wilmott2006paul} gives a broad treatment of implementation. In nearly all of this work, the test is numerical: how closely the grid solution matches an analytic or reference price. Whether the prices are good enough to trade after costs is rarely considered.

\subsection{Technical Indicators and Options}
RSI \cite{wilder1978new}, MACD \cite{appel2005technical} and Bollinger Bands \cite{bollinger2001bollinger} were designed for the price series of an asset. An option's premium also depends on time decay ($\Theta$) and on changes in implied volatility ($\nu$), so a correct call on the index can still lose money on the option. We do not claim that indicators are useless; we use them as the benchmark most retail traders actually apply.

\subsection{Transaction Costs in Indian Markets}
The SEBI study \cite{sebi2023study} documents how large costs are, relative to retail gross profits. Our cost model follows the same statutory components and combines them into a single-entry threshold.

\subsection{Gap}
We could not find a published comparison of PDE-based fair-value signals against retail indicator strategies on Indian index options with a full statutory cost model. This gap is addressed in this study by adding walk-forward and cost-stress tests to check how fragile the result is.

\section{Mathematical Formulation}

\subsection{The Black-Scholes-Merton PDE}
Under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$, the value $V(S,t)$ of a European option on an index $S$ with continuous dividend yield $q$ and risk-free rate $r$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + (r - q) S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - r V = 0
\label{eq:bsm_pde}
\end{equation}
with terminal condition at $t = T$
\begin{equation}
V(S, T) = \begin{cases}
\max(S - K, 0), & \text{call} \\
\max(K - S, 0), & \text{put}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the strike and $\sigma$ is the volatility. For a call, the spatial boundaries are $V(0, t) = 0$ and $V(S_{\max}, t) = S_{\max} - K e^{-r(T-t)}$. For a put they are $V(0, t) = K e^{-r(T-t)}$ and $V(S_{\max}, t) = 0$.

\subsection{Crank-Nicolson Discretization}
With time to maturity $\tau = T - t$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:bsm_pde}) becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau} = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + (r - q) S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - r V
\end{equation}

We split $S \in [0, S_{\max}]$ into $M$ steps of size $\Delta S = S_{\max} / M$, with $S_{\max} = \max(3K, 2.5 S_0)$, so that the upper boundary was well away from the region of interest. The time is split into $N$ steps of size $\Delta \tau = T / N$, and $V_i^j \approx V(i \Delta S, j \Delta \tau)$. Central differences give
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} \approx \frac{V_{i+1} - 2V_i + V_{i-1}}{\Delta S^2}, \quad \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \approx \frac{V_{i+1} - V_{i-1}}{2\Delta S}
\end{equation}
so at interior node $S_i = i \Delta S$ the spatial operator is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(V)_i = a_i V_{i-1} + b_i V_i + c_i V_{i+1}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
a_i &= \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 i^2 - \frac{1}{2}(r - q) i \\
b_i &= -(\sigma^2 i^2 + r) \\
c_i &= \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 i^2 + \frac{1}{2}(r - q) i
\end{align}

Crank--Nicolson averages the implicit ($j+1$) and explicit ($j$) levels:
\begin{equation}
\frac{V_i^{j+1} - V_i^j}{\Delta \tau} = \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}(V^{j+1})_i + \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}(V^j)_i
\end{equation}
which can be written as the tridiagonal system
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} \mathbf{V}^{j+1} = \mathbf{B} \mathbf{V}^j + \mathbf{b}^{j, j+1}
\label{eq:matrix_system}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are $(M-1) \times (M-1)$ matrices built from
\begin{align}
\alpha_i &= \frac{1}{4} \Delta \tau \left(\sigma^2 i^2 - (r - q) i\right) \\
\beta_i &= \frac{1}{2} \Delta \tau \left(\sigma^2 i^2 + r\right) \\
\gamma_i &= \frac{1}{4} \Delta \tau \left(\sigma^2 i^2 + (r - q) i\right)
\end{align}
$\mathbf{A}$ has diagonal $1 + \beta_i$, sub-diagonal $-\alpha_i$, and super-diagonal $-\gamma_i$. $\mathbf{B}$ has diagonal $1 - \beta_i$, sub-diagonal $\alpha_i$, and super-diagonal $\gamma_i$. The vector $\mathbf{b}^{j,j+1}$ carries the Dirichlet boundary values at $S = 0$ and $S = S_{\max}$.

\subsection{Time-Step Guard}
Crank--Nicolson is unconditionally stable in the von Neumann sense; however, with large time steps, we still observed oscillations near the boundaries. As a precaution we cap the step size at
\begin{equation}
\Delta \tau_{\max} = \frac{0.9 \cdot S_{\max}^2}{\sigma^2 M^2}
\end{equation}
and if $T/N$ exceeds it, we raise $N$ to $\lceil T / \Delta \tau_{\max} \rceil + 1$. We also floor $T$ at $10^{-6}$ years so that contracts on their expiry day do not cause a division by zero. The grid is $M = N = 300$. \fillin{authors: add the reason, e.g.\ the convergence test or runtime trade-off behind 300}.

\subsection{Pre-Factorization with SuperLU}
Within one pricing call, $\Delta S$, $\Delta \tau$, $\sigma$, and $r$ are fixed; therefore, $\mathbf{A}$ is the same at every step. We store it in compressed sparse column form and factorize it once with SuperLU \cite{li2005superlu}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{U}
\end{equation}
Each step then requires only a forward and backward substitution on a tridiagonal system, which costs $O(M)$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{V}^{j+1} = \mathbf{U}^{-1} \left(\mathbf{L}^{-1} \left(\mathbf{B} \mathbf{V}^j + \mathbf{b}\right)\right)
\end{equation}
We did not formally profile the solver. \fillin{authors: insert measured time per option chain with CPU model and chain size, or delete this sentence; do not state a latency figure that was not measured}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pde_solver} provides the full procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Crank-Nicolson PDE Option Pricer with SuperLU}
\label{alg:pde_solver}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $S_0, K, T, r, q, \sigma, M, N, \text{option\_type}$
\STATE $S_{\max} \leftarrow \max(3K,\; 2.5 S_0)$
\STATE $\Delta S \leftarrow S_{\max} / M$, \quad $\Delta \tau \leftarrow T / N$
\STATE Enforce time-step cap: if $\Delta \tau > 0.9 S_{\max}^2 / (\sigma^2 M^2)$ then increase $N$
\STATE Compute $\alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, M{-}1$
\STATE Build tridiagonal $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ in CSC sparse format
\STATE $\mathbf{A}_{\text{LU}} \leftarrow \texttt{splu}(\mathbf{A})$ \COMMENT{one-time factorization}
\STATE Initialize $\mathbf{V}^0$ from the payoff at $\tau = 0$
\FOR{$j = 0$ \TO $N-1$}
    \STATE $\text{rhs} \leftarrow \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{V}^j + \mathbf{b}^{j,j+1}$
    \STATE $\mathbf{V}^{j+1} \leftarrow \mathbf{A}_{\text{LU}}.\texttt{solve}(\text{rhs})$ \COMMENT{$O(M)$ per step}
    \STATE Apply boundary conditions at $S = 0$ and $S = S_{\max}$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\texttt{interp}(S_0, \; S_{\text{grid}}, \; \mathbf{V}^N)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Avoiding Circular Volatility}
If each strike is priced with its own implied volatility $\sigma_{\text{IV}}(K)$, the solver returns the market price up to a numerical error, and every mispricing is zero. To avoid this, we fit a natural cubic spline $\mathcal{S}_{\text{vol}}(K)$ through the implied volatilities of the strikes $K_1, \dots, K_p$ available that day, with $\mathcal{S}''_{\text{vol}}(K_1) = \mathcal{S}''_{\text{vol}}(K_p) = 0$. Each strike is then priced at
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{surface}}(K) = \mathcal{S}_{\text{vol}}(K)
\end{equation}
and the mispricing is
\begin{equation}
\delta_{\text{mispricing}}(K) = C_{\text{market}}(K) - C_{\text{PDE}}\left(K, \sigma_{\text{surface}}(K)\right)
\label{eq:mispricing}
\end{equation}
(with $P$ in place of $C$ for puts). A negative value means that the quote is below what the smooth surface implies, which we treat as a long signal. We chose a spline over SABR or SVI because it has no parameters to calibrate, which keeps the daily fit simple. The cost is that a spline can behave badly at the wings, and we restrict trading to near-ATM strikes for that reason (Section~V).

\subsection{Round-Trip Cost}
For a buy-then-sell round trip on an option with premium $P$ and lot size $L$, the cost per unit is
\begin{align}
\text{STT}_{\text{unit}} &= P \times 0.000625 \label{eq:stt} \\
\text{Exchange}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times P \times 0.000500 \\
\text{Stamp}_{\text{unit}} &= P \times 0.000030 \\
\text{SEBI}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times P \times 0.000001 \\
\text{Brokerage}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times 20.0 / L \\
\text{GST}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times (\text{Brokerage}_{\text{unit}}/2 + \text{Exchange}_{\text{unit}}/2) \times 0.18 \\
\text{Spread}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times P \times 0.005
\end{align}
and the total is
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{RT}}(P) = \sum_{\text{components}} \text{Component}_{\text{unit}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Entry Threshold}
Trade is entered only if the mispricing is larger than the cost of getting in and out, plus a margin.
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_t = F_{\text{RT}}(P_t) \times \kappa
\label{eq:epsilon}
\end{equation}
We set $\kappa = 1.5$ as a cushion for the price movement between the signal and fill. \fillin{authors: say how 1.5 was chosen, e.g., judgement or a sensitivity check; the code only shows it as a default}. A position is opened when $|\delta_{\text{mispricing}}| > \epsilon_t$ and the sign is favorable.

\section{Strategies}

All three strategies trade the same NIFTY~50 option universe under the same risk rules. Entries are limited to strikes within 150 index points of spot. Early in development, the spline produced large ``mispricings'' on deep in-the-money and far out-of-the-money contracts with distant expiries. These came from the fit at the edge of the strike range and not from real opportunities, and such contracts are illiquid anyway, so we cut them off.

\subsection{Common Risk Rules}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Position size:} At most 95\% of the current equity in one trade. An earlier version sized trades off a fixed notional and could allocate more than the account actually held after a drawdown.
    \item \textbf{Stop-loss:} exit if the premium falls 35\% below the entry.
    \item \textbf{Take-profit:} exit if the premium rises 60\% above the entry.
    \item \textbf{Maximum hold:} five trading days.
    \item \textbf{Minimum premium:} Rs.~10. Cheaper contracts lose too large a share of their value to costs and are noisy to price.
    \item \textbf{One position at a time.}
\end{itemize}
The 35\% and 60\% levels were fixed in advance and not tuned for the sample. \fillin{Authors: confirm this is true; if the levels were tuned, say so here}.

\subsection{Strategy 1: Retail Baseline (RSI + MACD)}
This is meant to represent a common retail rule and ignores the option's value.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Long call:} RSI(14) $\le 45$ and the MACD histogram (MACD line minus its 9-period signal line, 12/26 EMAs) are positive.
    \item \textbf{Long put:} RSI(14) $\ge 55$ and the MACD histogram are negative.
\end{itemize}
Exits are the stop-loss, the take-profit, or the 5-day limit. We make no claim that this is the best indicator rule; it is a simple, widely used one.

\subsection{Strategy 2: Pure PDE (Challenger~A)}
\textbf{Entry.} Buy a call if $C_{\text{market}} - C_{\text{PDE}} < -\epsilon_t$ and buy a put if $P_{\text{market}} - P_{\text{PDE}} < -\epsilon_t$, with $\epsilon_t$ from Eq.~(\ref{eq:epsilon}).

\textbf{Exit.} The first of:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the mispricing has shrunk to $|\delta| < \epsilon_t/2$, checked only after the position has been held for at least two bars.
    \item the 35\% stop-loss;
    \item the 60\% take-profit;
    \item the 5-day limit.
\end{enumerate}
The 2-bar minimum exists because of a problem encountered during testing. The surface is refitted every day, so a mispricing could disappear on the next bar simply because the spline moved, with no change in the option's price. The strategy then closed the trade at roughly zero gross profit and paid the full costs. We call these ghost trades, and the minimum hold removed them.

The idea behind the strategy is that an option quoted below its fair value relative to the surface will drift back toward it. We do not test this mechanism directly; we only observe whether trading on it made money.

\subsection{Strategy 3: Hybrid (Challenger~B)}
This adds momentum confirmation to the PDE entry. A call requires $C_{\text{market}} - C_{\text{PDE}} < -\epsilon_t$, a positive MACD histogram, and RSI $< 65$. A put requires $P_{\text{market}} - P_{\text{PDE}} < -\epsilon_t$, a negative MACD histogram, and RSI $> 35$. Exits are the same as those for Challenger~A. The motivation was to skip signals that fire against a strong trend. Section~7 shows that this mostly reduced the number of trades.

\section{Data and Backtest Design}

\subsection{Data}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Underlying:} daily NIFTY~50 OHLCV data from December 2023 to June 2024 (180 trading days), downloaded using \texttt{yfinance}. RSI-14, MACD (12/26/9), EMA-20/50, Bollinger Bands (20/2), and ATR-14 were computed from the data.
    \item \textbf{Options:} 1,980 strike-level records from NSE F\&O Bhavcopy archives, with price, implied volatility, open interest, and volume for calls and puts.
\end{itemize}
The period includes the run-up to the 2024 general election, RBI policy meetings, and both calm and volatile periods. It is still a six-month window.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Expiries.} The raw Bhavcopy mixes weekly, monthly, quarterly, and long-dated expiries; therefore, each daily cross-section is filtered to the expiry being traded.
    \item \textbf{Missing days.} When a Bhavcopy file is missing, for example on exchange holidays, we skip the day and do not forward-fill.
    \item \textbf{Zero-volume strikes} are dropped because their quotes may not be executable.
    \item \textbf{An ATM strike} is the strike closest to the day's closing spot.
    \item \textbf{Surface fit.} A natural cubic spline is fitted each day across the remaining strikes, and every strike is priced from it rather than from its own IV.
    \item \textbf{India VIX} closes are used to split the sample into regimes at the 33rd and 67th percentiles: low ($<12.6$), normal ($12.6$--$14.5$), and high ($>14.5$). If the VIX download fails, the pipeline returns an empty series, and the regime analysis is omitted.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Backtester}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The initial capital was INR~1,00,000, and the lot size was 50.
    \item Trades are executed at the daily close. The only execution cost besides statutory charges is a 0.5\% half-spread.
    \item Full round-trip costs come off every trade.
    \item Equity updates after each closed trade, and the next position is sized based on the current equity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why the Hybrid Did Worse}
Adding the momentum filter cut the return from $+49.57\%$ to $+7.85\%$. The hybrid took 31 trades against 67; therefore, it traded approximately half as often, and its win rate fell to 22.58\%. The filter therefore did not pick out better PDE trades; it mainly removed trades, and some of the removed ones were profitable. In this sample, momentum confirmation added nothing to the PDE signal.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}

On this sample, trading the gap between a Crank-Nicolson price and the market price, with the model volatility taken from a spline surface, beat an RSI/MACD strategy by a wide margin after Indian costs. The result held across VIX regimes and across four out-of-sample folds, and it survived costs three times the statutory level.

Several things limit how far these numbers can be pushed. The data cover six months and one market episode, and the PDE strategy made 67 trades; thus, the Sharpe ratios carry wide uncertainty, and we did not run any formal significance test. Trades are filled at the daily close, which ignores opening gaps and real order-book depth. The surface is a daily static spline, and a stochastic-volatility model, such as Heston or SABR, might handle high-VIX periods better. Several parameters (the 150-point window, $\kappa$, the stop and target levels, and the grid size) were set by judgement, and we have not shown how sensitive the results are to them.

The next steps include a multi-year dataset, a significance test against the baseline, a sensitivity study for the parameters above, and intraday or order-book data for more realistic fills. Extending the solver to American-style exercises and testing other high-cost exchanges are also open.
