\begin{abstract}
Derivatives trading on India's National Stock Exchange (NSE) is overwhelmingly dominated by retail participants relying on heuristic technical indicators such as the Relative Strength Index (RSI) and Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD), while quantitative institutional desks deploy continuous-time partial differential equation (PDE) valuation models. In this study, we investigate whether theoretical mispricing signals generated by an $O(M)$ SuperLU pre-factorized Crank-Nicolson PDE scheme can produce actionable multi-day alpha compared to conventional retail heuristics on NIFTY~50 index options.

We construct a smooth cubic-spline implied volatility surface to break the volatility circularity problem and incorporate a dynamic statutory friction model that accounts for Securities Transaction Tax (STT), NSE exchange turnover fees, stamp duty, GST, brokerage, and bid-ask spreads. Across a 180-day authentic empirical dataset sourced from NSE F\&O Bhavcopy archives (December 2023 -- June 2024), we compare a Retail Baseline strategy against a Pure PDE Statistical Arbitrage strategy and a Hybrid ``Quant-Mental'' Filter that combines PDE undervaluation with directional momentum confirmation.

Our results demonstrate that the Pure PDE strategy achieves a net return of $+49.57\%$ with an annualized Sharpe ratio of $2.07$ and Sortino ratio of $5.09$, decisively outperforming the Retail Baseline which returns $-5.58\%$ (Sharpe $= -0.40$). Regime-conditioned analysis reveals that PDE alpha persists across all India VIX environments, with peak performance during normal volatility (Sharpe $= 3.28$, $+21.44\%$). Walk-forward validation across four out-of-sample folds confirms robust generalization (Sharpe $> 2.0$ in all folds), and friction stress testing demonstrates resilience up to $3\times$ nominal statutory costs (Sharpe $= 1.43$, $+34.74\%$). These findings establish that continuous numerical PDE fair-pricing constitutes a viable alpha factor in emerging derivative markets, significantly outperforming retail technical heuristics under realistic statutory frictions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} National Stock Exchange of India (NSE) has emerged as the world's largest derivatives exchange by contract volume, with weekly and monthly index options on the NIFTY~50 and BANKNIFTY indices witnessing exponential retail trading activity. According to a landmark empirical study published by the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) in 2023 \cite{sebi2023study}, over 89\% of individual retail traders in equity derivatives incur net trading losses, with statutory transaction costs---Securities Transaction Tax (STT), exchange levies, stamp duty, and Goods and Services Tax (GST)---contributing substantially to the negative expectation of retail strategies. The average retail F\&O trader lost approximately INR~50,000 per year, with an additional 28\% erosion attributable purely to transaction costs.

The overwhelming majority of retail derivative market participants utilize empirical technical analysis heuristics---predominantly the Relative Strength Index \cite{wilder1978new}, Moving Average Convergence Divergence \cite{appel2005technical}, and Bollinger Bands \cite{bollinger2001bollinger}---to establish directional positioning in short-dated options. These indicators operate on historical price action alone and make no reference to the theoretical fair value of the derivative contract itself. Conversely, quantitative proprietary desks deploy continuous-time partial differential equation (PDE) solvers, such as the Crank-Nicolson finite difference scheme \cite{crank1947practical}, to derive exact theoretical fair values from the Black-Scholes-Merton framework \cite{black1973pricing, merton1973theory} and capture micro-structural mispricings between observed market prices and model-implied fair values.

While extensive literature evaluates numerical PDE solvers from a pure computational throughput or boundary accuracy perspective \cite{duffy2006finite, wilmott2006paul}, virtually no empirical literature investigates whether continuous numerical PDE fair-pricing can serve as an offensive alpha factor directly benchmarked against retail technical heuristics under realistic emerging market statutory frictions. This gap is particularly significant in the Indian context, where statutory transaction costs are among the highest globally for derivative instruments and constitute a material drag on any high-frequency or high-turnover strategy.

In this paper, we bridge this literature gap by introducing a complete quantitative framework consisting of four interconnected components:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cubic Spline Volatility Surface}: We construct a cross-sectional volatility smile using natural cubic splines fitted across available strike implied volatilities, supplying an independent model volatility parameter $\sigma_{\text{surface}}(K)$ that breaks the circularity of evaluating theoretical mispricing against the same market prices used to calibrate the model.
    \item \textbf{$O(M)$ SuperLU Factorization}: We leverage pre-factorized tridiagonal matrices in the Crank-Nicolson backward-stepping loop to achieve linear-time valuation per timestep, enabling real-time pricing of entire option chains within sub-millisecond latency.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Indian Statutory Friction Model}: We model exact SEBI-prescribed statutory costs---STT on sell-side premium turnover, NSE exchange charges, stamp duty on buy-side turnover, SEBI turnover fees, 18\% GST on taxable services, flat brokerage, and bid-ask half-spread impact---to derive a dynamic execution threshold $\epsilon_t$ that a mispricing signal must exceed before a trade is economically viable.
    \item \textbf{The Hybrid ``Quant-Mental'' Filter}: We evaluate whether augmenting theoretical PDE undervaluation signals with directional technical momentum confirmation (MACD histogram and RSI bounds) eliminates false mean-reversion entries in trending markets.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~II reviews related work. Section~III presents the mathematical formulation of the PDE solver and volatility surface. Section~IV details the Indian statutory friction model. Section~V describes the three strategy formulations and their risk management rules in detail. Section~VI describes the experimental dataset and backtesting methodology. Section~VII presents the empirical results, including regime analysis, walk-forward validation, and friction stress testing. Section~VIII presents visualizations of key findings. Section~IX concludes with practical implications and directions for future research.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Numerical PDE Methods in Option Pricing}
The application of finite difference methods to option pricing originates with Schwartz~\cite{schwartz1977valuation}, who first demonstrated the use of implicit finite differences to solve the Black-Scholes PDE for American options with early exercise boundaries. The Crank-Nicolson scheme, originally developed for heat conduction problems~\cite{crank1947practical}, was subsequently adopted in computational finance due to its second-order convergence in both space and time, providing a favorable balance between accuracy and computational cost~\cite{duffy2006finite}.

Wilmott~\cite{wilmott2006paul} provides comprehensive coverage of finite difference implementations in quantitative finance, including stability analysis and boundary condition treatment. However, the existing literature overwhelmingly focuses on numerical accuracy benchmarks (convergence rates, boundary condition errors, comparison with analytical Black-Scholes closed-form solutions) rather than evaluating whether the theoretical prices produced by these solvers can generate \textit{tradable alpha} in live markets after accounting for real-world transaction costs.

\subsection{Technical Analysis in Derivatives Trading}
The efficacy of technical indicators in equity and derivatives markets has been debated extensively. Wilder~\cite{wilder1978new} introduced the RSI as a momentum oscillator, while Appel~\cite{appel2005technical} formalized the MACD as a trend-following indicator. Bollinger~\cite{bollinger2001bollinger} proposed volatility bands as dynamic support/resistance levels. While these indicators have demonstrated value in trending equity markets, their application to short-dated options---where time decay ($\Theta$) and implied volatility dynamics ($\nu$) dominate price action---remains theoretically questionable. Options are derivative instruments whose value is governed by a stochastic PDE, not by historical price momentum alone.

\subsection{Transaction Cost Analysis in Indian Markets}
The SEBI study of 2023~\cite{sebi2023study} represents the most comprehensive analysis of retail derivative trading outcomes in India, documenting that statutory frictions alone consume a significant fraction of gross trading profits. Our friction model builds upon this regulatory framework by decomposing all seven statutory cost components into a unified dynamic threshold function, enabling precise assessment of strategy viability under authentic Indian market conditions.

\subsection{Research Gap}
To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has conducted a direct empirical comparison between numerical PDE fair-pricing signals and retail technical heuristics as competing alpha factors on Indian index options, under a fully specified statutory friction model with walk-forward out-of-sample validation. This paper addresses this gap.

\section{Mathematical Formulation}

\subsection{The Black-Scholes-Merton PDE}
Under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$, the value of a European option contract $V(S, t)$ on an underlying index $S$ with constant continuous dividend yield $q$ and risk-free interest rate $r$ satisfies the parabolic partial differential equation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + (r - q) S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - r V = 0
\label{eq:bsm_pde}
\end{equation}
subject to the terminal boundary condition at maturity $t = T$:
\begin{equation}
V(S, T) = \begin{cases} 
\max(S - K, 0), & \text{Call Options} \\
\max(K - S, 0), & \text{Put Options}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $K$ denotes the strike price and $\sigma$ is the volatility parameter. The spatial boundary conditions for a European call are $V(0, t) = 0$ and $V(S_{\max}, t) = S_{\max} - K e^{-r(T-t)}$; for a European put, $V(0, t) = K e^{-r(T-t)}$ and $V(S_{\max}, t) = 0$.

\subsection{Crank-Nicolson Discretization Scheme}
Transforming to forward time-to-maturity $\tau = T - t$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:bsm_pde}) becomes:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau} = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + (r - q) S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - r V
\end{equation}

We discretize the price domain $S \in [0, S_{\max}]$ into $M$ spatial steps with uniform step size $\Delta S = S_{\max} / M$, where $S_{\max} = \max(3K, 2.5 S_0)$ to ensure the computational domain adequately covers the region of interest. The time domain $\tau \in [0, T]$ is discretized into $N$ steps with $\Delta \tau = T / N$. We denote $V_i^j \approx V(i \Delta S, j \Delta \tau)$.

The spatial derivatives are approximated via second-order central differences:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} \approx \frac{V_{i+1} - 2V_i + V_{i-1}}{\Delta S^2}, \quad \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \approx \frac{V_{i+1} - V_{i-1}}{2\Delta S}
\end{equation}

For interior node $S_i = i \Delta S$, the spatial operator $\mathcal{L}(V)_i$ evaluates to:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(V)_i = a_i V_{i-1} + b_i V_i + c_i V_{i+1}
\end{equation}
where the coefficients are:
\begin{align}
a_i &= \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 i^2 - \frac{1}{2}(r - q) i \\
b_i &= -(\sigma^2 i^2 + r) \\
c_i &= \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 i^2 + \frac{1}{2}(r - q) i
\end{align}

The Crank-Nicolson scheme averages implicit ($j+1$) and explicit ($j$) temporal discretizations, yielding second-order convergence in both $\Delta S$ and $\Delta \tau$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{V_i^{j+1} - V_i^j}{\Delta \tau} = \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}(V^{j+1})_i + \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}(V^j)_i
\end{equation}

Rearranging into matrix form produces the tridiagonal linear system:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} \mathbf{V}^{j+1} = \mathbf{B} \mathbf{V}^j + \mathbf{b}^{j, j+1}
\label{eq:matrix_system}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are $(M-1) \times (M-1)$ tridiagonal matrices. The coefficient entries are:
\begin{align}
\alpha_i &= \frac{1}{4} \Delta \tau \left(\sigma^2 i^2 - (r - q) i\right) \\
\beta_i &= \frac{1}{2} \Delta \tau \left(\sigma^2 i^2 + r\right) \\
\gamma_i &= \frac{1}{4} \Delta \tau \left(\sigma^2 i^2 + (r - q) i\right)
\end{align}

Matrix $\mathbf{A}$ (implicit side) has main diagonal $(1 + \beta_i)$, sub-diagonal $-\alpha_i$, and super-diagonal $-\gamma_i$. Matrix $\mathbf{B}$ (explicit side) has main diagonal $(1 - \beta_i)$, sub-diagonal $\alpha_i$, and super-diagonal $\gamma_i$. The boundary correction vector $\mathbf{b}^{j,j+1}$ incorporates the Dirichlet conditions at $S = 0$ and $S = S_{\max}$.

\subsection{CFL Stability Guard}
Although the Crank-Nicolson scheme is unconditionally stable in the von Neumann sense, large timesteps can introduce oscillatory artifacts near boundary regions. We enforce a dynamic Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) stability criterion:
\begin{equation}
\Delta \tau_{\max} = \frac{0.9 \cdot S_{\max}^2}{\sigma^2 M^2}
\end{equation}
If the initial $\Delta \tau = T/N$ exceeds $\Delta \tau_{\max}$, the temporal resolution $N$ is automatically increased to $N_{\text{new}} = \lceil T / \Delta \tau_{\max} \rceil + 1$, guaranteeing smooth convergence without user intervention.

\subsection{SuperLU $O(M)$ Factorization}
Because grid spacing $\Delta S$, timestep $\Delta \tau$, volatility $\sigma$, and interest rate $r$ remain constant throughout the backward-stepping loop, Matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is time-invariant. We exploit this by converting $\mathbf{A}$ into Compressed Sparse Column (CSC) format and computing its LU factorization once using the SuperLU direct solver~\cite{li2005superlu} outside the temporal loop:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{U}
\end{equation}

Each subsequent time step $j \to j+1$ then executes in linear $O(M)$ asymptotic time via triangular forward/backward substitution:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{V}^{j+1} = \mathbf{U}^{-1} \left(\mathbf{L}^{-1} \left(\mathbf{B} \mathbf{V}^j + \mathbf{b}\right)\right)
\end{equation}

This optimization reduces the per-chain pricing latency to less than 2.5~ms for a 50-strike option chain on standard AMD Ryzen consumer hardware, enabling real-time deployment.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pde_solver} summarizes the complete pricing procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Crank-Nicolson PDE Option Pricer with SuperLU}
\label{alg:pde_solver}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $S_0, K, T, r, q, \sigma, M, N, \text{option\_type}$
\STATE $S_{\max} \leftarrow \max(3K,\; 2.5 S_0)$
\STATE $\Delta S \leftarrow S_{\max} / M$, \quad $\Delta \tau \leftarrow T / N$
\STATE Enforce CFL: if $\Delta \tau > 0.9 S_{\max}^2 / (\sigma^2 M^2)$ then increase $N$
\STATE Compute $\alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, M{-}1$
\STATE Build tridiagonal $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ in CSC sparse format
\STATE $\mathbf{A}_{\text{LU}} \leftarrow \texttt{splu}(\mathbf{A})$ \COMMENT{One-time SuperLU factorization}
\STATE Initialize $\mathbf{V}^0$ from terminal payoff at $\tau = 0$
\FOR{$j = 0$ \TO $N-1$}
    \STATE $\text{rhs} \leftarrow \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{V}^j + \mathbf{b}^{j,j+1}$
    \STATE $\mathbf{V}^{j+1} \leftarrow \mathbf{A}_{\text{LU}}.\texttt{solve}(\text{rhs})$ \COMMENT{$O(M)$ per step}
    \STATE Apply boundary conditions at $S = 0$ and $S = S_{\max}$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\texttt{interp}(S_0, \; S_{\text{grid}}, \; \mathbf{V}^N)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Resolution of the Volatility Circularity Problem}
A fundamental challenge in using PDE-derived fair values as trading signals is the \textit{volatility circularity problem}: if we use each strike's own market-implied volatility $\sigma_{\text{IV}}(K)$ as input to the PDE, the solver will reproduce the market price by construction, yielding zero mispricing everywhere. To break this circularity, we calibrate a natural cubic spline $\mathcal{S}_{\text{vol}}(K)$ across the cross-sectional strikes $K_1, \dots, K_p$ with natural boundary conditions $\mathcal{S}''_{\text{vol}}(K_1) = \mathcal{S}''_{\text{vol}}(K_p) = 0$.

The model fair value $C_{\text{PDE}}(K)$ is then computed using the smooth \textit{surface volatility}:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{surface}}(K) = \mathcal{S}_{\text{vol}}(K)
\end{equation}

This surface volatility represents a smoothed consensus view of implied volatility across the strike spectrum. Strike-level deviations of market prices from the surface-implied PDE prices reveal localized mispricings:
\begin{equation}
\delta_{\text{mispricing}}(K) = C_{\text{market}}(K) - C_{\text{PDE}}\left(K, \sigma_{\text{surface}}(K)\right)
\label{eq:mispricing}
\end{equation}

A negative $\delta_{\text{mispricing}}$ indicates that the market is underpricing the option relative to the smooth volatility consensus, presenting a potential long entry opportunity. The cubic spline approach is preferred over parametric models (e.g., SABR, SVI) because it requires no calibration hyperparameters and adapts naturally to the empirically observed strike structure of Indian index options.

In Indian equity derivative markets, trading frictions are non-trivial and constitute a material drag on strategy returns. Unlike developed markets where transaction costs are dominated by brokerage commissions and bid-ask spreads alone, Indian markets impose multiple layers of statutory charges prescribed by SEBI, the Government of India, and the NSE. All backtests in this study enforce the complete statutory cost structure detailed in Table~\ref{tab:friction}.

\subsection{Round-Trip Cost Decomposition}
For a complete round-trip trade (buy entry + sell exit) on an option with premium $P$ and lot size $L$, the total friction per unit of premium is computed as:
\begin{align}
\text{STT}_{\text{unit}} &= P \times 0.000625 \label{eq:stt} \\
\text{Exchange}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times P \times 0.000500 \\
\text{Stamp}_{\text{unit}} &= P \times 0.000030 \\
\text{SEBI}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times P \times 0.000001 \\
\text{Brokerage}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times 20.0 / L \\
\text{GST}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times (\text{Brokerage}_{\text{unit}}/2 + \text{Exchange}_{\text{unit}}/2) \times 0.18 \\
\text{Spread}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times P \times 0.005
\end{align}

The total round-trip friction per unit is:
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{RT}}(P) = \sum_{\text{components}} \text{Component}_{\text{unit}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dynamic Execution Threshold}
The dynamic execution threshold $\epsilon_t$ determines the minimum mispricing magnitude required to justify entering a trade:
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_t = F_{\text{RT}}(P_t) \times \kappa
\label{eq:epsilon}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa = 1.5$ is a conservative execution buffer multiplier that accounts for adverse price movement between signal generation and order execution (slippage). A position is entered only when the theoretical mispricing exceeds this threshold: $|\delta_{\text{mispricing}}| > \epsilon_t$.

\section{Strategy Formulations}

This section describes the three competing strategies evaluated in our empirical study. Each strategy operates on the same underlying NIFTY~50 index options universe and is subject to identical risk management constraints, ensuring a fair comparison. All strategies trade at-the-money (ATM) or near-ATM options within a 150-point strike window of the current spot price, restricting execution to the most liquid portion of the option chain where bid-ask spreads are tightest and the cubic spline volatility surface calibration is most reliable.

\subsection{Common Risk Management Framework}
All three strategies share the following position management rules to ensure consistent risk control:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Position Sizing}: Maximum 95\% of current portfolio equity allocated to any single trade, preventing over-concentration.
    \item \textbf{Stop-Loss}: A hard stop-loss is enforced at $-35\%$ of the option premium entry price. If the option's value declines by more than 35\% from entry, the position is immediately liquidated.
    \item \textbf{Take-Profit}: A take-profit target is set at $+60\%$ of the entry premium. Positions are closed upon reaching this threshold to lock in gains.
    \item \textbf{Maximum Holding Period}: All positions are closed after a maximum of 5 trading bars (days) regardless of profit or loss, preventing excessive exposure to time decay ($\Theta$).
    \item \textbf{Minimum Premium Filter}: Options with market premium below Rs.~10 are excluded from trade consideration, as these deep out-of-the-money contracts exhibit extreme gamma risk and unreliable pricing.
    \item \textbf{Single Position Constraint}: Only one active position is held at any time, simplifying portfolio accounting and avoiding correlation-induced drawdown amplification.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Strategy~1: Retail Baseline (RSI + MACD)}
The Retail Baseline represents the predominant approach used by Indian retail derivative traders, combining two of the most widely deployed technical indicators. This strategy makes no reference to the theoretical fair value of the option contract and relies entirely on historical price action of the underlying index.

\textbf{Entry Logic}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Long Call (Bullish Signal)}: The 14-period Relative Strength Index satisfies RSI(14) $\le 45.0$ \textit{and} the MACD Histogram (computed as MACD Line minus 9-period Signal Line from 12/26 EMA crossover) is positive (MACD$_{\text{hist}} > 0$). This combination identifies bullish momentum expansion where the underlying is not yet overbought.
    \item \textbf{Long Put (Bearish Signal)}: RSI(14) $\ge 55.0$ \textit{and} MACD$_{\text{hist}} < 0$. This identifies bearish momentum where the underlying is exhibiting downward pressure without being oversold.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Exit Logic}: Stop-loss ($-35\%$), take-profit ($+60\%$), or 5-bar time expiry, whichever triggers first.

\textbf{Rationale}: RSI measures the magnitude of recent price changes to evaluate overbought/oversold conditions, while MACD captures trend direction and momentum. Their combination is the single most common retail options trading heuristic in the Indian market.

\subsection{Strategy~2: Pure PDE Statistical Arbitrage (Challenger~A)}
The Pure PDE strategy executes strictly on theoretical mispricing signals derived from the Crank-Nicolson PDE solver relative to the calibrated cubic spline volatility surface. This strategy treats each option as a mathematical instrument whose fair value is governed by the Black-Scholes PDE, and enters positions only when the market price deviates sufficiently from the model price to overcome all transaction costs.

\textbf{Entry Logic}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Long Call}: The market is underpricing the call option relative to the PDE fair value---formally, $C_{\text{market}} - C_{\text{PDE}} < -\epsilon_t$, where $\epsilon_t$ is the dynamic friction threshold from Eq.~(\ref{eq:epsilon}).
    \item \textbf{Long Put}: $P_{\text{market}} - P_{\text{PDE}} < -\epsilon_t$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Exit Logic}: The position is closed when any of the following conditions is met:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mean Reversion}: After a minimum holding period of 2 bars (to prevent premature exit due to daily surface recalibration noise), the mispricing has reverted to $|\delta| < \epsilon_t / 2$.
    \item \textbf{Stop-Loss}: Premium declines by $-35\%$ from entry.
    \item \textbf{Take-Profit}: Premium appreciates by $+60\%$ from entry.
    \item \textbf{Time Expiry}: 5-bar maximum holding period reached.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Near-ATM Restriction}: Only strikes within 150 index points of the current spot price are considered for trade entry. Deep in-the-money and deep out-of-the-money options are excluded because the cubic spline surface exhibits higher fitting error at the wings, where liquidity is thin and bid-ask spreads are wide. This constraint eliminates phantom mispricings caused by poor surface calibration at extreme moneyness levels.

\textbf{Rationale}: If the PDE solver accurately estimates fair value, a negative mispricing (market price below fair value) represents an undervalued option that should revert toward its theoretical value as market participants discover the dislocation. The minimum 2-bar hold prevents the strategy from being whipsawed by the daily recalibration of the volatility surface, which can cause the mispricing signal to vanish on the next bar even though no actual price convergence has occurred.

\subsection{Strategy~3: Hybrid Quant-Mental Filter (Challenger~B)}
The Hybrid strategy requires simultaneous alignment of two orthogonal signal sources: (1)~quantitative PDE undervaluation from the Crank-Nicolson solver, and (2)~directional trend momentum from technical indicators. The hypothesis is that combining model-driven fair-value signals with market-sentiment confirmation should eliminate false mean-reversion entries that occur in strongly trending markets where mispricings may persist or widen rather than revert.

\textbf{Entry Logic}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Long Call}: $(C_{\text{market}} - C_{\text{PDE}} < -\epsilon_t)$ \textit{and} (MACD$_{\text{hist}} > 0$) \textit{and} (RSI $< 65.0$).
    \item \textbf{Long Put}: $(P_{\text{market}} - P_{\text{PDE}} < -\epsilon_t)$ \textit{and} (MACD$_{\text{hist}} < 0$) \textit{and} (RSI $> 35.0$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Exit Logic}: Identical to Challenger~A (mean reversion, stop-loss, take-profit, or time expiry).

\textbf{Rationale}: The momentum filters (MACD direction and RSI bounds) are intended to screen out PDE undervaluation signals that occur in markets trending against the signal direction. In practice, as our results will demonstrate, this additional filtering proves overly conservative and eliminates too many valid PDE signals, reducing the strategy's trade count and overall alpha capture.

\section{Experimental Design and Data}

\subsection{Dataset Construction}
Our empirical study uses authentic market data sourced from two primary channels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Underlying OHLCV Data}: 180 trading days of NIFTY~50 daily open-high-low-close-volume data spanning December 2023 through June 2024, retrieved via the \texttt{yfinance} Python package. Technical indicators (RSI-14, MACD 12/26/9, EMA-20/50, Bollinger Bands 20/2, ATR-14) are computed from this series.
    \item \textbf{Option Chain Cross-Sections}: 1,980 individual strike-level option chain records extracted from NSE F\&O Bhavcopy daily archives, containing market prices, implied volatilities, open interest, and volume for both call and put contracts across the available strike grid for each trading day.
\end{itemize}

The dataset period encompasses multiple distinct market regimes, including the 2024 Indian general election volatility surge, RBI monetary policy announcements, and both low-VIX consolidation and high-VIX expansion periods, providing a representative cross-section of Indian market conditions.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
Several preprocessing steps ensure data integrity:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Zero-Volume Strike Filtering}: Strikes with zero traded volume on a given day are excluded, as their quoted prices may not reflect executable levels.
    \item \textbf{ATM Identification}: For each trading day, the at-the-money strike is identified as the strike closest to the closing spot price of the NIFTY~50 index.
    \item \textbf{Volatility Surface Calibration}: For each trading day, a natural cubic spline is fitted across all available strike implied volatilities to generate the $\sigma_{\text{surface}}(K)$ surface. The PDE solver then prices each strike using this surface volatility rather than the individual strike's own IV.
    \item \textbf{India VIX Integration}: Daily India VIX closing values are used for regime segmentation. VIX tercile boundaries are computed at the 33rd and 67th percentiles of the sample distribution ($\text{VIX} < 12.6$: Low; $12.6 \le \text{VIX} \le 14.5$: Normal; $\text{VIX} > 14.5$: High).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Backtesting Methodology}
The backtesting engine operates in a deterministic, event-driven fashion:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial Capital}: INR~1,00,000 (Rs.~1~Lakh).
    \item \textbf{Lot Size}: 50 units per contract (NIFTY standard lot).
    \item \textbf{Execution Assumption}: Trades execute at the daily close price with no additional slippage beyond the modeled bid-ask half-spread (0.5\%).
    \item \textbf{Friction Application}: Full round-trip statutory friction is deducted from every trade's net P\&L.
    \item \textbf{Equity Tracking}: Portfolio equity is updated after each closed trade, and subsequent position sizing is computed against the \textit{current} equity, not the initial capital.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analysis of the Hybrid Filter's Underperformance}
The Hybrid strategy (Challenger~B) underperforms the Pure PDE strategy ($+7.85\%$ vs.\ $+49.57\%$) despite incorporating additional signal confirmation. For near-ATM mispricing capture in index options, the PDE signal alone is sufficiently robust and does not require directional confirmation. The momentum filter introduces a ``confirmation tax'' that reduces capital deployment without improving the quality of selected trades.

\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Summary of Contributions}
This study presents the first rigorous empirical benchmark of continuous Crank-Nicolson PDE solvers against conventional retail technical heuristics on Indian index options under a fully specified statutory friction model. Our principal findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Decisive PDE Alpha}: Numerical PDE fair pricing---when properly constrained to near-ATM, near-month contracts with adequate position sizing---generates robust, regime-invariant alpha (Sharpe $= 2.07$, net return $+49.57\%$) that decisively outperforms standard retail momentum indicators (Sharpe $= -0.40$, net return $-5.58\%$).
    \item \textbf{Regime Invariance}: Unlike momentum indicators that perform inconsistently across volatility regimes, the PDE strategy generates positive Sharpe ratios in low, normal, and high VIX environments, with peak performance during normal volatility (Sharpe $= 3.28$).
    \item \textbf{Friction Resilience}: The PDE strategy remains profitable with Sharpe $> 1.4$ even under $3\times$ nominal statutory friction, confirming that the mispricing edge substantially exceeds real-world transaction costs.
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Sample Robustness}: Walk-forward validation across four consecutive folds confirms Sharpe $> 2.0$ in every out-of-sample period, ruling out overfitting.
    \item \textbf{Momentum Filtering is Counterproductive}: The Hybrid strategy's underperformance demonstrates that PDE mispricing signals are self-sufficient for near-ATM index options and do not benefit from directional momentum confirmation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Practical Implications}
These findings carry direct implications for both retail traders and quantitative practitioners operating in the Indian derivatives ecosystem:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Retail traders relying exclusively on RSI/MACD for options entry timing face a structurally negative expectation after accounting for Indian statutory frictions.
    \item The $O(M)$ SuperLU-optimized Crank-Nicolson solver enables real-time deployment on consumer hardware (sub-2.5~ms per 50-strike chain), making numerical PDE pricing accessible beyond institutional infrastructure.
    \item The cubic spline volatility surface provides a simple, hyperparameter-free approach to breaking the IV circularity problem that is practical for daily recalibration workflows.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study evaluates multi-day holding periods using daily close prices; intraday microstructure effects (e.g., opening auction dynamics, intraday volatility clustering) are not captured. Second, the cubic spline surface assumes a static volatility environment within each trading day; incorporating stochastic volatility models (Heston, SABR) may improve surface stability during high-VIX regimes. Third, the dataset spans 180 trading days; extending the analysis to multi-year periods encompassing structural market shifts would strengthen the robustness conclusions. Finally, the strategies are restricted to single-leg directional positions; multi-leg structures (vertical spreads, iron condors) may offer improved risk-adjusted returns by limiting maximum loss exposure.

Future research directions include: (a)~extending the PDE framework to American-style options with early exercise boundaries, (b)~incorporating real-time order book depth data for more precise spread estimation, (c)~evaluating intraday execution on tick-level data from broker WebSocket feeds, and (d)~testing the framework on other emerging market derivative exchanges with high statutory friction environments.
