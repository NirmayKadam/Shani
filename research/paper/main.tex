\begin{abstract}
Index options on the National Stock Exchange of India (NSE) are traded mostly by retail participants, and most of them rely on indicators such as the Relative Strength Index (RSI) and Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD). Institutional desks price the same contracts with model-based fair values. We ask a narrow question: if we price NIFTY~50 options against a smooth volatility surface and trade the gap between model and market price, does that gap survive Indian transaction costs, and how does it compare with an RSI/MACD strategy?

Each strike is priced from a natural cubic-spline volatility surface fitted across strikes, with a forward implied by put-call parity; a Crank-Nicolson solver of the Black-Scholes-Merton equation provides the reference formulation. Costs are modelled explicitly: date-dependent STT, exchange and SEBI charges, stamp duty, GST, brokerage and a bid-ask allowance. The data are the full NIFTY option chain rebuilt from NSE F\&O Bhavcopy files, 860 sessions from January 2021 to June 2024, split into four periods that are each backtested independently with next-session fills and daily marking of every position on its own contract.

The mispricing strategy (Pure PDE) made \PureTradesTotal{} trades with a mean net return of $\PureRoPMean\%$ of premium per trade; the momentum-filtered Hybrid returned $\HybridRoPMean\%$ and the RSI/MACD Retail Baseline $\RetailRoPMean\%$. Only \CellsPositive{} of the 12 strategy--period combinations were profitable, the bootstrap confidence interval for the mean return per trade lay above zero in \CellsCIAboveZero{} of them, and none was consistently better than a matched random-entry baseline. A delta-hedged test shows that options flagged as cheap do recover relative to other options overnight, but after a realistic fill the remaining edge is INR~\SigUnderHoneExcess{} per unit, below the INR~\SigCost{} round-trip cost. At daily frequency and after Indian costs, neither the model-based signal nor the indicator rule produced a tradeable edge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{N}{SE} is the largest derivatives exchange in the world by contract count, and weekly NIFTY~50 and BANKNIFTY options account for a large share of that volume. SEBI's 2023 study of individual traders \cite{sebi2023study} found that approximately 89\% of them lost money in equity derivatives. The average loss was roughly INR~50,000 per trader, and transaction costs added about 28\% on top of it. These costs include Securities Transaction Tax (STT), exchange levies, stamp duty, and GST.

Most of these traders work from chart indicators: RSI \cite{wilder1978new}, MACD \cite{appel2005technical} and Bollinger Bands \cite{bollinger2001bollinger}. An indicator reads the past path of the underlying and does not indicate whether the option itself is cheap or expensive. Pricing desks start at the opposite end. They compute the fair value from the Black-Scholes-Merton framework \cite{black1973pricing, merton1973theory}, often with finite-difference methods such as Crank-Nicolson \cite{crank1947practical}, and compare it with the quote.

The finite-difference literature \cite{duffy2006finite, wilmott2006paul} is mostly about accuracy: convergence order, boundary treatment, and grid design. We did not find a study that takes the resulting model-versus-market gap, charges it the full set of Indian statutory costs, and compares the outcome with a plain retail indicator strategy. This paper is a small attempt at that comparison.

The system grew out of a few problems we ran into along the way. Our first version priced each option with its own implied volatility, and the model price came back equal to the market price every time, so there was nothing to trade. We now fit a natural cubic spline to the day's implied volatilities across strikes and price each strike from that curve, $\sigma_{\text{surface}}(K)$; deviations from the curve are what we call mispricing. The solver is Crank-Nicolson with the system matrix factorized once using SuperLU, since the matrix does not change within a pricing call. On the cost side, we coded the STT, exchange, stamp duty, SEBI, GST, and brokerage rules one by one, added a bid-ask allowance, and used the total as a per-trade threshold $\epsilon_t$ that a mispricing has to exceed before we enter. Finally, we tried a hybrid variant that also asks MACD and RSI to agree, to see whether that removes PDE signals firing against a strong trend.

To make the comparison fair we test on three and a half years of the full option chain, run each period as an independent backtest, fill every signal at the next session's close, and judge each strategy by its return per trade with confidence intervals and against a random-entry baseline, not only against zero.

An earlier version of the project also included a CNN-LSTM price predictor and a FinBERT sentiment module. We removed both and kept a purely model-based pipeline because \fillin{authors: state the real reason, e.g., results were not reproducible, signals could not be explained, latency}.

The remainder of this paper covers related work (Section~II), the solver and volatility surface (III), the cost model (IV), the strategies (V), data and backtester (VI), results (VII), and conclusions (VIII).

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Finite-Difference Methods in Option Pricing}
Schwartz \cite{schwartz1977valuation} was among the first to solve the option-pricing PDE with implicit finite differences. The Crank-Nicolson scheme was developed for heat conduction \cite{crank1947practical} and later became a standard tool in finance because it is second-order accurate in both space and time at modest cost \cite{duffy2006finite}. Wilmott \cite{wilmott2006paul} gives a broad treatment of implementation. In nearly all of this work, the test is numerical: how closely the grid solution matches an analytic or reference price. Whether the prices are good enough to trade after costs is rarely considered.

\subsection{Technical Indicators and Options}
RSI \cite{wilder1978new}, MACD \cite{appel2005technical} and Bollinger Bands \cite{bollinger2001bollinger} were designed for the price series of an asset. An option's premium also depends on time decay ($\Theta$) and on changes in implied volatility ($\nu$), so a correct call on the index can still lose money on the option. We do not claim that indicators are useless; we use them as the benchmark most retail traders actually apply.

\subsection{Transaction Costs in Indian Markets}
The SEBI study \cite{sebi2023study} documents how large costs are, relative to retail gross profits. Our cost model follows the same statutory components and combines them into a single-entry threshold.

\subsection{Evaluating Trading Strategies}
Bailey et al.\ \cite{bailey2017probability} show how easily a backtest that looks good in sample fails out of sample, and Harvey, Liu and Zhu \cite{harvey2016cross} argue that the bar for significance must rise with the number of tests. We therefore report every strategy--period combination and use bootstrap confidence intervals \cite{efron1993bootstrap} rather than a single headline return.

\subsection{Gap}
We could not find a published comparison of PDE-based fair-value signals against retail indicator strategies on Indian index options with a full statutory cost model and a multi-year, out-of-sample test. This study addresses that gap.

\section{Mathematical Formulation}

\subsection{The Black-Scholes-Merton PDE}
Under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$, the value $V(S,t)$ of a European option on an index $S$ with continuous dividend yield $q$ and risk-free rate $r$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + (r - q) S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - r V = 0
\label{eq:bsm_pde}
\end{equation}
with terminal condition at $t = T$
\begin{equation}
V(S, T) = \begin{cases}
\max(S - K, 0), & \text{call} \\
\max(K - S, 0), & \text{put}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the strike and $\sigma$ is the volatility. For a call, the spatial boundaries are $V(0, t) = 0$ and $V(S_{\max}, t) = S_{\max} - K e^{-r(T-t)}$. For a put they are $V(0, t) = K e^{-r(T-t)}$ and $V(S_{\max}, t) = 0$.

\subsection{Crank-Nicolson Discretization}
With time to maturity $\tau = T - t$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:bsm_pde}) becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau} = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + (r - q) S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - r V
\end{equation}

We split $S \in [0, S_{\max}]$ into $M$ steps of size $\Delta S = S_{\max} / M$, with $S_{\max} = \max(3K, 2.5 S_0)$, so that the upper boundary was well away from the region of interest. The time is split into $N$ steps of size $\Delta \tau = T / N$, and $V_i^j \approx V(i \Delta S, j \Delta \tau)$. Central differences give
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} \approx \frac{V_{i+1} - 2V_i + V_{i-1}}{\Delta S^2}, \quad \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \approx \frac{V_{i+1} - V_{i-1}}{2\Delta S}
\end{equation}
so at interior node $S_i = i \Delta S$ the spatial operator is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(V)_i = a_i V_{i-1} + b_i V_i + c_i V_{i+1}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
a_i &= \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 i^2 - \frac{1}{2}(r - q) i \\
b_i &= -(\sigma^2 i^2 + r) \\
c_i &= \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 i^2 + \frac{1}{2}(r - q) i
\end{align}

Crank--Nicolson averages the implicit ($j+1$) and explicit ($j$) levels:
\begin{equation}
\frac{V_i^{j+1} - V_i^j}{\Delta \tau} = \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}(V^{j+1})_i + \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}(V^j)_i
\end{equation}
which can be written as the tridiagonal system
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} \mathbf{V}^{j+1} = \mathbf{B} \mathbf{V}^j + \mathbf{b}^{j, j+1}
\label{eq:matrix_system}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are $(M-1) \times (M-1)$ matrices built from
\begin{align}
\alpha_i &= \frac{1}{4} \Delta \tau \left(\sigma^2 i^2 - (r - q) i\right) \\
\beta_i &= \frac{1}{2} \Delta \tau \left(\sigma^2 i^2 + r\right) \\
\gamma_i &= \frac{1}{4} \Delta \tau \left(\sigma^2 i^2 + (r - q) i\right)
\end{align}
$\mathbf{A}$ has diagonal $1 + \beta_i$, sub-diagonal $-\alpha_i$, and super-diagonal $-\gamma_i$. $\mathbf{B}$ has diagonal $1 - \beta_i$, sub-diagonal $\alpha_i$, and super-diagonal $\gamma_i$. The vector $\mathbf{b}^{j,j+1}$ carries the Dirichlet boundary values at $S = 0$ and $S = S_{\max}$.

\subsection{Time-Step Guard}
Crank--Nicolson is unconditionally stable in the von Neumann sense; however, with large time steps, we still observed oscillations near the boundaries. As a precaution we cap the step size at
\begin{equation}
\Delta \tau_{\max} = \frac{0.9 \cdot S_{\max}^2}{\sigma^2 M^2}
\end{equation}
and if $T/N$ exceeds it, we raise $N$ to $\lceil T / \Delta \tau_{\max} \rceil + 1$. We also floor $T$ at $10^{-6}$ years so that contracts on their expiry day do not cause a division by zero. \fillin{authors: state the grid size used and the reason, e.g.\ the convergence test or runtime trade-off}.

\subsection{Pre-Factorization with SuperLU}
Within one pricing call, $\Delta S$, $\Delta \tau$, $\sigma$, and $r$ are fixed; therefore, $\mathbf{A}$ is the same at every step. We store it in compressed sparse column form and factorize it once with SuperLU \cite{li2005superlu}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{U}
\end{equation}
Each step then requires only a forward and backward substitution on a tridiagonal system, which costs $O(M)$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{V}^{j+1} = \mathbf{U}^{-1} \left(\mathbf{L}^{-1} \left(\mathbf{B} \mathbf{V}^j + \mathbf{b}\right)\right)
\end{equation}
We did not formally profile the solver. \fillin{authors: insert measured time per option chain with CPU model and chain size, or delete this sentence; do not state a latency figure that was not measured}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pde_solver} provides the full procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Crank-Nicolson PDE Option Pricer with SuperLU}
\label{alg:pde_solver}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $S_0, K, T, r, q, \sigma, M, N, \text{option\_type}$
\STATE $S_{\max} \leftarrow \max(3K,\; 2.5 S_0)$
\STATE $\Delta S \leftarrow S_{\max} / M$, \quad $\Delta \tau \leftarrow T / N$
\STATE Enforce time-step cap: if $\Delta \tau > 0.9 S_{\max}^2 / (\sigma^2 M^2)$ then increase $N$
\STATE Compute $\alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, M{-}1$
\STATE Build tridiagonal $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ in CSC sparse format
\STATE $\mathbf{A}_{\text{LU}} \leftarrow \texttt{splu}(\mathbf{A})$ \COMMENT{one-time factorization}
\STATE Initialize $\mathbf{V}^0$ from the payoff at $\tau = 0$
\FOR{$j = 0$ \TO $N-1$}
    \STATE $\text{rhs} \leftarrow \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{V}^j + \mathbf{b}^{j,j+1}$
    \STATE $\mathbf{V}^{j+1} \leftarrow \mathbf{A}_{\text{LU}}.\texttt{solve}(\text{rhs})$ \COMMENT{$O(M)$ per step}
    \STATE Apply boundary conditions at $S = 0$ and $S = S_{\max}$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\texttt{interp}(S_0, \; S_{\text{grid}}, \; \mathbf{V}^N)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Avoiding Circular Volatility}
If each strike is priced with its own implied volatility $\sigma_{\text{IV}}(K)$, the model returns the market price up to a numerical error, and every mispricing is zero. To avoid this, we fit a natural cubic spline $\mathcal{S}_{\text{vol}}(K)$ through the implied volatilities of the strikes $K_1, \dots, K_p$ available that day, with $\mathcal{S}''_{\text{vol}}(K_1) = \mathcal{S}''_{\text{vol}}(K_p) = 0$. Each strike is then priced at
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{surface}}(K) = \mathcal{S}_{\text{vol}}(K)
\end{equation}
and the mispricing is
\begin{equation}
\delta_{\text{mispricing}}(K) = C_{\text{market}}(K) - C_{\text{model}}\left(K, \sigma_{\text{surface}}(K)\right)
\label{eq:mispricing}
\end{equation}
(with $P$ in place of $C$ for puts). A negative value means that the quote is below what the smooth surface implies, which we treat as a long signal. We chose a spline over SABR or SVI because it has no parameters to calibrate, which keeps the daily fit simple. The cost is that a spline can behave badly at the wings, and we restrict trading to near-ATM strikes for that reason (Section~V). Because the spline is fitted through the call implied volatilities, it reproduces call prices almost exactly (largest call mispricing INR~\LegacyCallMispricingMax), so in practice the signal comes from puts: their deviation from the surface defined by the calls.

\subsection{Valuation Used in the Backtest}
Two details of the valuation matter as much as the solver. First, pricing from the index level with $q = 0$ ignores the dividend yield and the futures basis, which shifts all put prices relative to calls; we therefore value every option on the forward $F$ implied by put-call parity at the at-the-money strike, $F = K + (C - P)e^{rT}$, using Black's formula for options on a forward \cite{black1976pricing}. Second, the Crank-Nicolson grid has a discretization error of INR~\GridErrMean{} on average (up to INR~\GridErrMax) relative to the closed-form price, which is of the same size as the entry threshold and would otherwise be traded as signal. The backtest therefore uses the closed-form price; the PDE solver of this section reproduces it up to that grid error and remains the general tool for contracts without a closed form.

\subsection{Round-Trip Cost}
For a buy-then-sell round trip on an option with premium $P$ and position size $L$ units, the cost per unit is
\begin{align}
\text{STT}_{\text{unit}} &= P \times \text{STT rate on the sell date} \label{eq:stt} \\
\text{Exchange}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times P \times 0.000500 \\
\text{Stamp}_{\text{unit}} &= P \times 0.000030 \\
\text{SEBI}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times P \times 0.000001 \\
\text{Brokerage}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times 20.0 / L \\
\text{GST}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times (\text{Brokerage}_{\text{unit}}/2 + \text{Exchange}_{\text{unit}}/2) \times 0.18 \\
\text{Spread}_{\text{unit}} &= 2 \times P \times 0.005
\end{align}
and the total is
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{RT}}(P) = \sum_{\text{components}} \text{Component}_{\text{unit}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Entry Threshold}
Trade is entered only if the mispricing is larger than the cost of getting in and out, plus a margin.
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_t = F_{\text{RT}}(P_t) \times \kappa
\label{eq:epsilon}
\end{equation}
We set $\kappa = 1.5$ as a cushion for the price movement between the signal and fill. \fillin{authors: say how 1.5 was chosen, e.g., judgement or a sensitivity check; the code only shows it as a default}. A position is opened when $|\delta_{\text{mispricing}}| > \epsilon_t$ and the sign is favorable.

\section{Strategies}

All three strategies trade the same NIFTY~50 option universe under the same risk rules. Entries are limited to strikes within 150 index points of spot. Early in development, the spline produced large ``mispricings'' on deep in-the-money and far out-of-the-money contracts with distant expiries. These came from the fit at the edge of the strike range and not from real opportunities, and such contracts are illiquid anyway, so we cut them off.

\subsection{Common Risk Rules}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Position size:} 50 units per trade, and at most 95\% of the current equity in one trade.
    \item \textbf{Stop-loss:} exit if the premium falls 35\% below the entry.
    \item \textbf{Take-profit:} exit if the premium rises 60\% above the entry.
    \item \textbf{Maximum hold:} five trading days, and every position is closed before its expiry day.
    \item \textbf{Minimum premium:} Rs.~10. Cheaper contracts lose too large a share of their value to costs and are noisy to price.
    \item \textbf{One position at a time.}
\end{itemize}
The 35\% and 60\% levels were fixed in advance and not tuned for the sample. \fillin{Authors: confirm this is true; if the levels were tuned, say so here}.

\subsection{Strategy 1: Retail Baseline (RSI + MACD)}
This is meant to represent a common retail rule and ignores the option's value.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Long call:} the MACD histogram (MACD line minus its 9-period signal line, 12/26 EMAs) is positive and RSI(14) is above 50 or at most 45.
    \item \textbf{Long put:} the MACD histogram is negative and RSI(14) is below 50 or at least 55.
\end{itemize}
In effect the rule follows the sign of the MACD histogram. Exits are the stop-loss, the take-profit, or the 5-day limit. We make no claim that this is the best indicator rule; it is a simple, widely used one.

\subsection{Strategy 2: Pure PDE (Challenger~A)}
\textbf{Entry.} Buy a call if $C_{\text{market}} - C_{\text{model}} < -\epsilon_t$ and buy a put if $P_{\text{market}} - P_{\text{model}} < -\epsilon_t$, with $\epsilon_t$ from Eq.~(\ref{eq:epsilon}), taking the first qualifying strike in ascending order.

\textbf{Exit.} The first of:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the mispricing has shrunk to $|\delta| < \epsilon_t/2$, checked only after the position has been held for at least two bars.
    \item the 35\% stop-loss;
    \item the 60\% take-profit;
    \item the 5-day limit.
\end{enumerate}
The 2-bar minimum exists because of a problem encountered during testing. The surface is refitted every day, so a mispricing could disappear on the next bar simply because the spline moved, with no change in the option's price. The strategy then closed the trade at roughly zero gross profit and paid the full costs. We call these ghost trades, and the minimum hold removed them.

The idea behind the strategy is that an option quoted below its fair value relative to the surface will drift back toward it. We test this mechanism directly in Section~VII-E.

\subsection{Strategy 3: Hybrid (Challenger~B)}
This adds momentum confirmation to the PDE entry. A call requires $C_{\text{market}} - C_{\text{model}} < -\epsilon_t$, a positive MACD histogram, and RSI $< 65$. A put requires $P_{\text{market}} - P_{\text{model}} < -\epsilon_t$, a negative MACD histogram, and RSI $> 35$. Exits are the same as those for Challenger~A. The motivation was to skip signals that fire against a strong trend.

\section{Data and Backtest Design}

\subsection{Data}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Options:} NSE F\&O Bhavcopy files for all 860 sessions from 1 January 2021 to 28 June 2024, giving every NIFTY option contract with its closing and settlement price, contracts traded and open interest. We keep strikes within 12\% of spot and expiries up to 60 days, about one million contract-days.
    \item \textbf{Underlying:} daily NIFTY~50 closes and India VIX from Yahoo Finance; RSI-14 and MACD (12/26/9) are computed from the closes.
\end{itemize}
The sample covers the post-COVID rally of 2021, the rate-hiking cycle of 2022, the calmer markets of 2023 and the run-up to the 2024 general election.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Expiry.} Signals are taken on the nearest expiry with at least 14 days to run, so that a next-session fill and a five-day hold end well before expiry. The surface is fitted for every expiry between 2 and 45 days, so a held contract keeps its own mispricing after the signal expiry moves on.
    \item \textbf{Stale prices.} For a contract with no trades on a day, the Bhavcopy closing price is a stale carried-forward value; such prices are never used for implied volatilities, fills or marks.
    \item \textbf{Missing days.} When a Bhavcopy file is missing, we skip the day and do not forward-fill.
    \item \textbf{An ATM strike} is the strike closest to the day's closing spot.
    \item \textbf{Surface fit.} A natural cubic spline is fitted each day across the traded strikes, and every strike is priced from it rather than from its own IV.
    \item \textbf{Rates} follow the RBI repo-rate schedule by date; positions are a fixed 50 units, so lot-size changes do not affect sizing.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Backtester}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Periods.} The sample is split into Period A (2021), B (2022), C (January--September 2023) and D (October 2023 -- June 2024). Each strategy is backtested in each period independently from INR~1,00,000, so a loss in one period cannot change the trades of another.
    \item \textbf{Fills.} A signal computed at the close of session $t$ is filled at the close of session $t+1$, on the same contract and only if that contract traded.
    \item \textbf{Marking.} Every open position is marked daily on its own contract (strike and expiry); equity reflects those marks and each trade's own costs.
    \item \textbf{Costs.} Full round-trip costs (Section~IV) come off every trade.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation.} Besides return, Sharpe ratio (RBI repo rate as hurdle) and drawdown, we report the mean and median net return on premium per trade with a bootstrap 95\% confidence interval, the return without the single largest trade, and the percentile of each result within \RandomDraws{} runs of a random baseline that buys near-ATM puts with the same fills, exits and costs and the same number of trades.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Does the Mispricing Signal Contain Information?}
A losing long-option strategy does not by itself show that the mispricing is uninformative: the signal is a relative-value gap, while an unhedged long put also carries index direction and time decay. We therefore measured each signal directly, as the delta-hedged change in the option price,
\begin{equation}
h_{t \to t+1} = \big(P_{t+1} - P_t\big) - \Delta_F(t)\,\big(F_{t+1} - F_t\big),
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_F$ is Black's put delta with respect to the forward and $F = S e^{rT}$ from the index close. Because hedged long options lose time value whatever the signal, the comparison is with all near-ATM puts in the same period (Table~\ref{tab:signal_v2}, Fig.~\ref{fig:signal}).

The signal does contain information. From the signal close to the next close, puts flagged as cheap beat the other puts by INR~\SigUnderHzeroExcess{} per unit and puts flagged as expensive trail them by INR~$\SigRichHzeroExcess$. However, by the time a position can be opened at the next close most of this has gone: the remaining excess is INR~\SigUnderHoneExcess{} per unit, below the INR~\SigCost{} round-trip cost. The mispricing behaves like short-lived noise in closing prices rather than a lasting deviation that can be traded at daily frequency.

\subsection{Why the Hybrid Did Not Help}
Adding the momentum filter reduced the number of trades from \PureTradesTotal{} to \HybridTradesTotal{} and moved the mean return per trade from $\PureRoPMean\%$ to $\HybridRoPMean\%$, but its interval still includes zero in every period, and its best period depends on one trade. The filter mainly removed trades rather than selecting better ones.

\section{Conclusion}

Over three and a half years of NIFTY~50 options, with every period tested independently, next-session fills and the full Indian cost structure, trading the gap between a surface-based model price and the market price did not beat a simple RSI/MACD strategy in any consistent way, and neither of them beat a random-entry baseline. The mispricing signal is real but small and short-lived: options that look cheap do recover against other options overnight, but not by enough to pay for the round trip once the position can actually be opened.

Several limitations remain. Fills are at the daily close and ignore the order book; intraday data could show whether the overnight reversion can be captured earlier, though it would face the same costs. The spline passes through the call implied volatilities, so calls carry almost no signal by construction; a surface fitted independently of the price being tested, such as SVI fitted jointly to calls and puts, is a natural next step. Several parameters (the 150-point window, $\kappa$, the stop and target levels) were set by judgement and deliberately not tuned on the results.

The next steps include intraday data, an independently fitted volatility surface, and extending the comparison to BANKNIFTY and to American-style exercise. For retail traders, the practical message is that neither indicators nor a model-based fair value is enough to overcome Indian transaction costs at daily frequency.
